Assignment 3a: Final report on clinical trial
QT correction is used to account for the dependence of the QT interval on heart rate. QTcF (Fridericia-corrected QT) corrects the QT interval using the cube root of the RR interval, whereas QTcB (Bazett-corrected QT) uses the square root of the RR interval. The protocol (Section 7.2.3) specifies that QTc would be calculated using both Bazett's (QTcB) and Fridericia's (QTcF) methods, yet the analysis dataset provided only QTcF. To explore the impact of this omission, I derived QTcB manually from the reported QTcF values and the continuously recorded heart rate (Vital Connect Healthpatch), matching each sparse ECG timepoint to the nearest heart rate reading.
Mathematically, QTcB = QTcF / RR interval^(1/6), since Bazett's correction (RR interval^0.5) and Fridericia's (RR interval^(1/3)) differ only in the exponent applied to the RR interval (the time between consecutive heartbeats, derived from heart rate as RR interval = 60/HR). This means that whenever a drug changes heart rate, QTcB and QTcF will diverge, and the size of that divergence depends directly on how much the RR interval has shifted. This is exactly what the derived data show: metoprolol (Active) lowered mean heart rate substantially relative to placebo (e.g. 56 vs 64 bpm at 118 min post-dose), lengthening the RR interval. Under Fridericia's correction the QTc change from baseline in the Active arm stayed close to zero (+2.0 ms), but under Bazett's correction the same subjects' QTc appeared to drop by 8.3 ms — a purely mathematical artefact of the RR interval^(1/6) term, not a real difference in cardiac repolarisation. 
This illustrates precisely why Fridericia's correction is considered superior for drugs that themselves alter heart rate, as beta-blockers do. Bazett's formula assumes a steeper QT–RR interval relationship (exponent 0.5) than is empirically observed (closer to 1/3), so it systematically under-corrects at slower heart rates and over-corrects at faster ones. When the study drug is also the cause of the heart-rate change, this bias is not random noise but a confound that inflates or deflates the apparent QTc effect in a predictable direction, which is why most modern QT/QTc studies favour QTcF (or subject-specific correction) over QTcB, and likely why only QTcF was retained in the final analysis dataset despite the protocol specifying both. My own reconstruction of QTcB, rather than filling a gap, ends up demonstrating why the QTcB is omitted.
